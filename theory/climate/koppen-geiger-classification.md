# The Köppen–Geiger Classification of Climates, and the Climate Diagram

## 1. A Climate as a Point, and a Class as a Region

Köppen classified climates by the vegetation they allow. His insight was that a few thresholds on temperature and precipitation separate the great biomes: rainforest from savannah, steppe from desert, forest from tundra. The classification has been revised many times since 1900. Its current standard is that of Peel et al. (2007), which Beck et al. (2018, 2023) used for maps at 1 km, from 1901 to 2099.

A *climate*, in this sense, is a normal year: the twelve monthly mean temperatures T = (T₁, …, T₁₂), in °C, and the twelve monthly precipitation totals P = (P₁, …, P₁₂), in mm. It is a point of ℝ¹² × ℝ¹²₊. The classification is a function from this space to a set of 30 classes, written by codes such as Cfb. Its fibres partition the space of climates.

Every condition below is a linear inequality in T and P, once it is known which month is the warmest, the driest, and so on. Each class is therefore a finite union of convex polyhedra, a *semilinear* set. Its boundaries are where a forecast of climate change can move a place from one class to another.

## 2. The Quantities

*Hypotheses.* T and P are normals of a stated period (here 1991–2020). The mean temperature of a month is the mean of its daily mean temperatures. Where only the means of the daily maxima and minima are known, as in TerraClimate, it is taken as their mean (an approximation good to a few tenths of a degree in most climates).

**Definition 1 (half-years).** Let A = {April, …, September} and O = {October, …, March}. *Summer* S is whichever of A and O has the larger sum of temperatures; *winter* W is the other. A tie, a climate without seasons, is resolved as S = A.

**Definition 2 (quantities; Beck et al. 2018, Table 2).**
- MAT = (1/12) Σ Tᵢ, the mean annual temperature; MAP = Σ Pᵢ, the annual precipitation.
- T_hot = max Tᵢ and T_cold = min Tᵢ; T_mon10 = #{i : Tᵢ > 10 °C}.
- P_dry = min Pᵢ; P_sdry and P_swet, the least and greatest Pᵢ over i ∈ S; P_wdry and P_wwet over W.
- P_S = Σ_{i∈S} Pᵢ and P_W = Σ_{i∈W} Pᵢ.
- The aridity threshold, in mm:

  P_th = 2 MAT if P_W > 0.7 MAP; 2 MAT + 28 if P_S > 0.7 MAP; 2 MAT + 14 otherwise.  (1)

*Remark.* A climate whose rain falls in winter, when evaporation is weak, needs less rain to escape aridity than one whose rain falls in summer. The three cases of (1) express this.

## 3. The Rules

The first letter is the main group, the second the regime of precipitation (or, in B, desert or steppe), and the third the temperature.

| Class | Condition |
|---|---|
| **B** arid | MAP < 10 P_th; then W (desert) if MAP < 5 P_th, else S (steppe); h if MAT ≥ 18, else k |
| **A** tropical | not B, and T_cold ≥ 18; f if P_dry ≥ 60; m if not f and P_dry ≥ 100 − MAP/25; w otherwise |
| **E** polar | not B, and T_hot ≤ 10; T if T_hot > 0, F otherwise |
| **C** temperate | not B, T_hot > 10 and 0 < T_cold < 18 |
| **D** cold | not B, T_hot > 10 and T_cold ≤ 0 |
| second letter of C and D | s if P_sdry < 40 and P_sdry < P_wwet/3; w if P_wdry < P_swet/10; f if neither. If both, s when P_W > P_S, w otherwise |
| third letter of C and D | a if T_hot ≥ 22; b if not a and T_mon10 ≥ 4; for D, d if not a or b and T_cold < −38; c otherwise |

Three choices distinguish this from Köppen (1936):
- C and D are separated at 0 °C rather than −3 °C (Russell 1931);
- the desert and steppe thresholds depend on the season of the rain, through (1);
- s and w are made exclusive by the season with more rain.

Group B takes precedence over all others (Beck et al. 2018, Methods). A cold, dry plateau with no month above 10 °C is therefore a cold steppe or desert, not tundra.

## 4. The Symmetry of the Classification

The rules never refer to the calendar. They refer only to the two half-years, and they tell the halves apart only by temperature. This has a precise consequence.

**Proposition 1.** Let G be the group generated by:
- permutations of the temperatures within A and within O;
- independent permutations of the precipitations within A and within O;
- the exchange of A and O, applied to temperature and precipitation together.

Then G ≅ (S₆ × S₆) ≀ ℤ/2, of order 2 · (6!)⁴. For every climate whose half-years differ in their sums of temperature, the class is constant on the G-orbit of (T, P).

*Proof.* Each quantity of Definition 2 is one of the following:
- a symmetric function of all twelve temperatures (MAT, T_hot, T_cold, T_mon10);
- a symmetric function of all twelve precipitations (MAP, P_dry);
- a symmetric function of the precipitations within the summer, or within the winter (P_S, P_sdry, P_swet, and those of W).

Permutations within a half leave all of these unchanged. They also leave the sums of temperature over each half unchanged, and hence which half is summer. The exchange of A and O maps the summer of (T, P) to the summer of the image when the sums differ, so it permutes the quantities of S and W consistently. Since the rules are functions of the quantities alone, the class is invariant. ∎

*Remark.*
- In particular, the shift by six months, which takes a northern climate to its southern twin, preserves the class. It is the exchange of A and O that matches April with October, May with November, and so on.
- The class is thus a function on the orbit space (ℝ¹² × ℝ¹²₊)/G. It sees the year only as two unordered seasons.
- The climate diagram (§5) is not invariant. It shows the order of the months, which the classification forgets.
- The tests check both invariances on 4000 random climates and random elements of G (`tests/run-tests.mjs`).

## 5. The Climate Diagram of Walter and Lieth

Walter and Lieth (1960) drew each station's normal year in one figure:
- months along the axis, from July in the southern hemisphere, so that summer is in the middle;
- temperature on the left scale and precipitation on the right, with 10 °C level with 20 mm.

The choice of scale is Gaussen's index of drought (Bagnouls and Gaussen 1957): a month is dry when P ≤ 2T. A dry season appears where the precipitation curve falls below the temperature curve, and is dotted. A humid season, where the precipitation curve lies above, is hatched. Above 100 mm the precipitation scale is reduced tenfold, and the very wet season is filled. Months whose mean daily minimum is below 0 °C, when frost is certain, are marked under the axis.

The atlas keeps one length per division in every diagram, so diagrams can be compared: a monsoon diagram is tall, a desert diagram short.

## 6. Verification

1. **Rules.** Fifteen constructed climates each meet one rule, including the precedence of B over E and both resolutions of the s–w conflict. All take their stated classes. Random climates reach all thirty classes.
2. **Maps.** Beck et al.'s 1991–2020 map places seven cities (Paris, Cairo, Singapore, Moscow, Rome, Mumbai, Tokyo) in their classes. The legend of the published maps lists the classes in the order the code numbers them.
3. **Agreement.** The atlas classifies the TerraClimate normals of 1991–2020 (Abatzoglou et al. 2018), sampled every 1/3°, and compares the result with Beck et al.'s map at 0.1°:
   - the class agrees in 89.1 % of 195 203 land cells;
   - the main group agrees in 95.9 %.

   The two rest on different climatologies, at different resolutions, so agreement is expected rather than identity. The differences lie in mountains and along the boundaries between classes. There, the climate is near a threshold, such as T_cold ≈ 0 °C between Cfa and Dfa at Denver.

## 7. Climate Classes Through Time

Beck et al. (2023) also map the classes of 1901–1930, 1931–1960 and 1961–1990, from observations. They map 2041–2070 and 2071–2099 under four scenarios of emissions, from CMIP6 models constrained by the observed warming. The atlas draws any of these maps. It can also show where a map differs from another: changed cells are coloured, unchanged ones faint.

*Caveat.*
- A future map shows the classes of the ensemble-mean climate. It does not show the spread among models, which Beck et al. give as a separate confidence.
- A change of class may be a small change of climate near a threshold, or a large one across a whole class. The map does not distinguish the two; the climate diagram of the place does.
