## Key Question 1: Classical vessel enhancement ("vesselness") — formulas, parameters, comparisons

### Takeaway
Hessian-eigenvalue filters (Sato 1998, Frangi 1998, Jerman 2016) remain the backbone of classical enhancement; they are cheap (one 3×3 symmetric eigenproblem per voxel per scale) but share known failure modes at bifurcations, near adjacent bright structures and on blobs. Optimally oriented flux (Law & Chung 2008) replaces the Hessian by gradient flux through a sphere's boundary, computed exactly by FFT, and is more robust to closely adjacent structures; RORPO (Merveille 2018) is a non-Hessian, morphological alternative. A reproducible C++ benchmark of seven filters on liver CT, brain MRA and synthetic data exists (Lamy et al., ICPR 2020/2021 and IEEE TMI 2022) and reports that bifurcations are often missed and that default parameters are often inadequate.

**Frangi (1998): formula as implemented, with the paper's equation numbers**
- Eigenvalues of the Gaussian-derivative Hessian at scale σ are sorted by magnitude, |λ1| ≤ |λ2| ≤ |λ3|. In 3D: R_A = |λ2|/|λ3| (paper eq. 11, plate vs line), R_B = |λ1|/√(|λ2λ3|) (eq. 10, blob vs line), S = √(Σλi²) (eq. 12, "structureness"); in 2D R_B = |λ1|/|λ2| (eq. 15) — [scikit-image v0.24 ridges.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-image/scikit-image/v0.24.0/skimage/filters/ridges.py)
- Response V = (1 − exp(−R_A²/2α²)) · exp(−R_B²/2β²) · (1 − exp(−S²/2c²)), defaults α = β = 0.5, and c (called γ in scikit-image) = max(S)/2 when not given — [scikit-image ridges.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-image/scikit-image/v0.24.0/skimage/filters/ridges.py)
- Sign handling in scikit-image: `black_ridges=True` (dark vessels) is the default; bright vessels are handled by negating the image; λ2, λ3 are clamped to ≥ 1e-10 (i.e. wrong-sign responses are suppressed) — [scikit-image ridges.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-image/scikit-image/v0.24.0/skimage/filters/ridges.py). The original paper's rule (V = 0 if λ2 > 0 or λ3 > 0 for bright vessels on a dark background) and its γ-normalised derivatives (σ^γ scaling) are **[unverified here]** — not re-read in this session.
- scikit-image computes the Hessian with `use_gaussian_derivatives=True` and, per the fetched code, applies **no σ² scaling** in `frangi` (but does in `sato`) — [scikit-image ridges.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-image/scikit-image/v0.24.0/skimage/filters/ridges.py). Implementations thus differ in scale normalisation; the Lamy TMI abstract notes the benchmark provides "up-to-date C++ implementations" for exactly this reason of comparability — [Semantic Scholar abstract of Lamy 2022](https://doi.org/10.1109/TMI.2022.3192679)

**Sato (1998), as implemented**
- scikit-image: after sorting, the largest eigenvalue is dropped and the response is σ² · (∏ max(λi, 0))^(1/(n−1)) over the remaining eigenvalues (dark-ridge convention), maximised over σ — [scikit-image ridges.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-image/scikit-image/v0.24.0/skimage/filters/ridges.py). This is a simplified geometric-mean variant; Sato's original line measure with its asymmetry parameters (α1, α2) is **[unverified here]**.

**Meijering (2004), as implemented**
- Eigenvalues are mixed by a circulant matrix with off-diagonal α (code default α = 1/(ndim+1), while the docstring says −1/(ndim+1) — an internal inconsistency), the largest-magnitude mixed value is kept, clipped at 0, and normalised per σ by its maximum — [scikit-image ridges.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-image/scikit-image/v0.24.0/skimage/filters/ridges.py)

**Jerman (2016), as implemented by the author**
- Regularised third eigenvalue: λρ = λ3 where λ3 > τ·max(λ3); λρ = τ·max(λ3) where 0 < λ3 ≤ τ·max(λ3); λρ = 0 where λ3 ≤ 0 (max taken over the volume at that scale); τ ∈ [0.5, 1] "controls response uniformity" — [vesselness3D.m](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/timjerman/JermanEnhancementFilter/master/vesselness3D.m)
- Response V = λ2²(λρ − λ2) · 27/(λ2 + λρ)³; V = 1 where λ2 ≥ λρ/2 > 0; V = 0 where λ2 ≤ 0 or λρ ≤ 0; non-finite values set to 0. Here λ2, λ3 are the (sign-flipped for bright-on-dark) cross-sectional eigenvalues; Hessian scaled by c = σ²; multiscale maximum, then divided by the global maximum and thresholded below 1e-2 — [vesselness3D.m](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/timjerman/JermanEnhancementFilter/master/vesselness3D.m)
- The function is "Based on code by D. Kroon, University of Twente (May 2009)"; the file carries no licence text and the GitHub API reports `NOASSERTION` for the repository — [GitHub API](https://github.com/timjerman/JermanEnhancementFilter)

**Optimally oriented flux (Law & Chung 2008), from the paper's text**
- Outward oriented flux along direction ρ̂ through the sphere ∂S_r of radius r: f(x; r, ρ̂) = ∫_{∂S_r} ((v(x+h)·ρ̂)ρ̂)·n̂ dA (eq. 1) = ρ̂ᵀ Q_{r,x} ρ̂ (eq. 2), with q^{ij}_{r,x} = ∫_{∂S_r} v_i(x + r n̂) n_j dA (eq. 3); v is the image gradient from first derivatives of a Gaussian with σ = 1 voxel — [Law & Chung 2008 PDF](https://home.cse.ust.hk/~achung/eccv08_law_chung.pdf)
- By the divergence theorem Q is a convolution, q^{ij} = ψ_{r,i,j} ∗ I with ψ_{r,i,j} = b_r ∗ g_{âiâj,σ} (eqs. 4–7; b_r the ball indicator), computed by FFT in O(N log N) via the closed form Ψ_{r,i,j}(u) = 4πr u_i u_j e^{−2(π‖u‖σ)²} ‖u‖^{−2} (cos(2πr‖u‖) − sin(2πr‖u‖)/(2πr‖u‖)) (eq. 8), u in cycles/voxel on [−0.5, 0.5) — [Law & Chung 2008 PDF](https://home.cse.ust.hk/~achung/eccv08_law_chung.pdf)
- Eigenvalues λ1 ≤ λ2 ≤ λ3 of Q (eqs. 9–11); inside a bright tube λ1 ≤ λ2 ≪ 0 and λ3 ≈ 0; just outside a tube λ3 ≫ 0. Λ12 = λ1 + λ2 is proposed as a centreline response for circular cross-sections — [Law & Chung 2008 PDF](https://home.cse.ust.hk/~achung/eccv08_law_chung.pdf)
- Multiscale: radii sampled every 0.5 voxel ("Nyquist"), linearly; eigenvalues divided by 4πr² (sphere area) before any multiscale aggregation, making the response scale-invariant — [Law & Chung 2008 PDF](https://home.cse.ust.hk/~achung/eccv08_law_chung.pdf)
- Stated advantage: Hessian responses (second derivatives of a Gaussian) average intensity differences over a region extending beyond the vessel and "can be affected by the adjacent objects"; OOF responds only where the sphere's surface touches the boundary, so it is "robust against the disturbance introduced by closely located adjacent structures" — [Law & Chung 2008 PDF](https://home.cse.ust.hk/~achung/eccv08_law_chung.pdf)
- The ITK implementation note (Insight Journal) states the tubularity is the sum of the N − 1 cross-sectional eigenvalues, normalised across scales — [search summary of insight-journal.org/pdfs/885.pdf](https://insight-journal.org/pdfs/885.pdf) [secondary; not read directly]

### Inferences
- For a C++ implementation from scratch, Frangi/Sato/Jerman reduce to: Gaussian second derivatives (separable convolutions), a closed-form or Jacobi 3×3 symmetric eigensolver per voxel, a per-scale measure, σ²-normalisation and a max over σ. OOF needs, per radius, six FFT-domain filter multiplications (the six distinct entries of the symmetric Q) with the closed-form Ψ of eq. 8, then the same 3×3 eigensolver; both map onto the infrastructure's calx (FFT, symmetric eigenproblem).
- Because implementations disagree on scale normalisation (scikit-image's `frangi` vs `sato`) and on sign conventions, a from-scratch implementation should be checked against an Apache-2.0 reference (ITK) or the Lamy C++ benchmark, not against scikit-image alone.
- Jerman's MATLAB code has no licence: reimplement from the paper (TMI 2016) rather than translating the code.
- The Jerman regularisation (λρ) is precisely aimed at Frangi's known weaknesses (non-uniform response across vessel cross-section and at bifurcations, where λ3 shrinks); OOF aims at adjacency; neither is designed for tumours/blobs in liver CT, which is where the Lamy benchmark is relevant.

## Key Question 2: Classical segmentation and tracking, and the major reviews

### Takeaway
The classical toolbox is: thresholding/region growing on a vesselness map; statistical mixture models of the intensity histogram (especially for TOF-MRA); deformable models (geodesic active contours, codimension-two CURVES, capillary/flux-driven flows); minimal paths by fast marching (Cohen–Kimmel, Deschamps–Cohen; 4D paths with radius, Li–Yezzi); centreline tracking (ridge traversal, Aylward–Bullitt; multiple-hypothesis template tracking, Friman); and graph cuts (Boykov–Jolly). Lesage et al. (2009) is the canonical review of 3D lumen segmentation; Kirbas & Quek (2004) and Moccia et al. (2018) bracket it; a liver-specific review exists (Ciecholewski & Kassjański 2021).

**What each computes (summary; papers not re-read in this session)**
- Geodesic active contours: a curve/surface minimising ∫ g(|∇I|) ds, i.e. a geodesic in a Riemannian metric weighted by an edge-stopping function g, evolved as a level set; CURVES extends this to codimension-two curves (thin vessels as 1D objects in 3D), the motivation being that surface-curvature flows shrink thin tubes (summary of [Caselles 1997](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1007979827043) and [Lorigo 2001](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1361-8415(01)00040-8)).
- Minimal paths: given a potential P > 0 (e.g. 1/(ε + vesselness)), the weighted distance U from a seed solves the eikonal equation |∇U| = P, computed by fast marching in O(N log N); the path is recovered by gradient descent on U; this gives a globally optimal centreline between two points, at the cost of "shortcuts" across weak edges (summary of [Cohen & Kimmel 1997](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1007922224810), [Deschamps & Cohen 2001](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1361-8415(01)00046-9)). Li & Yezzi lift the path to (x, y, z, r) so that the radius is optimised jointly (per the paper's title, [Li & Yezzi 2007](https://doi.org/10.1109/TMI.2007.903696)).
- Ridge traversal: from a seed, step along the tube direction (the Hessian eigenvector of smallest |λ|), re-centre on the intensity ridge in the normal plane, and adapt scale; the paper addresses initialisation, noise, singularities (branchings) and scale explicitly (per title, [Aylward & Bullitt 2002](https://doi.org/10.1109/42.993126)).
- Mixture models for TOF-MRA: the histogram is modelled as a mixture of background (e.g. Rayleigh/Gaussian) and vessel classes and fitted by EM, giving a threshold or posterior map; Wilson & Noble's is "adaptive" for TOF data, Hassouna et al.'s is "stochastic" (per titles, [Wilson & Noble 1999](https://doi.org/10.1109/42.811277), [Hassouna 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.media.2004.11.009)).
- Graph cuts: a binary labelling minimising a sum of regional (likelihood) and boundary (contrast) terms by s–t max-flow, with hard seeds; globally optimal for the given energy (summary of [Boykov & Jolly 2001](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICCV.2001.937505)). Known vessel failure mode: the boundary term favours short cuts, shrinking thin vessels unless the metric or flux terms are added.

### Inferences
- A from-scratch C++ classical pipeline that matches the literature's typical design: (1) multiscale vesselness or OOF; (2) seeded region growing / hysteresis threshold or graph cut on the vesselness-augmented energy; (3) centreline by minimal paths (fast marching) or skeletonisation; (4) radius estimation per centreline point (from the maximising scale or OOF radius). Steps (1)–(3) are each a self-contained, testable module.
- For TOF-MRA specifically, a histogram mixture model (EM) is a cheap baseline that the deep-learning literature still compares against; for PC-MRA and 4D flow, segmentation can also use velocity magnitude/coherence, which the classical TOF methods do not.
- Max-flow implementations: the widely used Boykov–Kolmogorov library has had research-only/GPL licensing in its history [unverified here]; for an MIT codebase, writing push–relabel or using an Apache/BSD solver would need a licence check.

### Gaps

## Key Question 3: Deep learning — established networks, vessel-specific networks, topology-preserving losses, and foundation models (licences and vessel accuracy)

### Takeaway
nnU-Net (Apache-2.0) remains the reference baseline, and the 2024 "revisited" study argues that well-configured CNN U-Nets are still not beaten by transformer/Mamba variants. Vessel-specific gains come mainly from topology-aware losses (clDice, Betti matching, Skeleton Recall, cbDice — all MIT or Apache-2.0). Among foundation models, only vesselFM targets vessels across modalities, and its code is GPL-3.0 and its weights research/non-commercial (Open RAIL++-M); its zero-shot Dice on MSD hepatic vessels is only ~30%. General promptable models (SAM-Med3D, MedSAM-2, VISTA3D) score very low zero-shot on vessels in vesselFM's comparison. TotalSegmentator gives Apache-2.0 weights for large named vessels and for lung and liver vessel tasks, but its coronary task is licensed.

## Key Question 4: Evaluation — metrics and inter-observer variability for vessels

### Takeaway
Volumetric Dice is dominated by large vessels and ignores connectivity, so vessel work pairs it with centreline/topology metrics (clDice, Betti-number errors or Betti matching error, centreline overlap à la Schaap 2009) and surface distances (HD95, ASSD). Challenges use task-specific protocols: VESSEL12 used ROC/AUC on annotated points; Parse2022 used multi-level Dice and HD95; TopCoW scored class-wise Dice, topology and graph/variant classification. Hard figures on inter-observer variability for 3D vessel CT/MR were scarce in what could be retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- Maier-Hein L, Reinke A, Godau P, et al. (2024). ### Inferences
- A complete vessel evaluation module should compute: Dice (and per-diameter-class Dice, following VESSEL12 and Lamy's diameter classes), clDice from skeletons, Betti-0/Betti-1 errors (or the Betti matching error via the MIT-licensed implementation), HD95/ASSD on surfaces, and, where centrelines exist, a Schaap-style centreline overlap. These are all computable in C++ from binary masks plus a skeletoniser and a connected-component/homology routine (morphe already holds homology over ℤ/2).
- Inter-observer Dice for large vessels (aorta ~0.96) is far above that for small vessels; reporting Dice per diameter class is therefore necessary to compare a method with human agreement.

Only a few vessel datasets carry a standard open licence permitting commercial use: TotalSegmentator v1/v2 CT and TotalSegmentator MRI (CC BY 4.0), AVT aortic vessel tree (CC BY 4.0 on Figshare, but with source-collection licences applying), and MSD (CC BY-SA 4.0 per the MSD, with an AWS page inconsistency). IXI (raw TOF-MRA, no vessel labels) is CC BY-SA 3.0. TopCoW is "open use, cite the source, commercial use requires permission". 3D-IRCADb-01 is CC BY-NC-ND 4.0. Most challenge datasets (ImageCAS, ASOCA, Parse2022, KiPA22, CAS2023, VESSEL12, SMILE-UHURA) are released under registration or data-use agreements without a standard licence. Several Zenodo records with CC BY-ND licences are challenge *proposal documents*, not the data — do not read their licence as the data licence.

### Inferences
- For tests in the infrastructure (`tests/fixtures/` hold small reference values, datasets go to `data/cache/`, never committed), the cleanest choices are TotalSegmentator v2 (CC BY 4.0; large named vessels, portal/splenic vein), AVT (CC BY 4.0 with source caveats), MSD Task08 (CC BY-SA 4.0; share-alike affects redistributed derivatives, not code) and IXI (CC BY-SA 3.0; raw TOF-MRA for classical filters, no labels). TopCoW is usable for research with attribution; commercial use needs permission.
- 3D-IRCADb-01 (NC-ND) and the agreement-gated challenge sets (ImageCAS, ASOCA, Parse2022, KiPA22, CAS2023, VESSEL12) should be fetched by the user under their terms, never mirrored, and derived masks should not be redistributed.
- The repeated pattern of CC BY-ND licences on Zenodo challenge records (Parse2022, KiPA22, CAS2023) are MICCAI challenge proposal PDFs; misreading them as data licences is an easy error.
